How to use this

  1. Pick the exchange your ship is at and how much cash you have.
  2. Enter your ship's cargo space and the fuel numbers from the flight planner (SFC).
  3. Add any gateway fees you pay, then press Find trades.
  4. Before you fly a trade, press Log this trade so you can compare the prediction with what you really make.

Your settings and trade log stay in this browser. Nothing is sent anywhere except requests for public market data to FIO.

Ship calibration:

Faster flights burn more fuel, and slower ones leave the cargo exposed to the market for longer. To weigh one against the other, the tool needs to know how your ship's flight time and fuel change with the two sliders in the flight planner. The game doesn't publish this, but planning a flight in SFC is free and shows both, so you can measure it in about ten minutes.

How to measure

  1. Load your ship with a full cargo and the SF you normally carry. Cargo weight changes STL speed.
  2. Open SFC and plan, but don't fly, a trip between two exchanges. For each plan, copy the flight time, the SF and FF it needs, and the two slider values into a row below.
  3. Plan the same trip at a few settings. Move one slider at a time: try the STL slider at 25%, 50% and 100%, then the reactor at 25%, 50% and 100%.
  4. Plan a second trip of a different length at one or two settings, so the tool can tell per-parsec time from fixed time.
  5. Press Fit model. From then on, Find trades picks the slider settings for each trip when this ship is selected.

Six or more flights work best. The tool only picks settings between the lowest and highest values you planned at, so it never guesses outside what you measured. Times can be typed as 6h 20m, 6:20 or 6.33.

    How it works

    Prosperous Universe is a small, self-contained version of physical commodity trading. This tool looks at a cargo the way a commodity trader would: buy where a good is cheap, pay to move it, sell where it is dear, but only if the spread survives every cost and the risk of the price moving while it travels.

    1. Real prices, not quotes

    It walks each exchange's order book level by level. Buying a lot of something moves you up the ask ladder, and that slippage often kills a spread that looks good on screen. Other players keep trading while you fly, so the bids you are counting on thin out. The share still there on arrival is e−0.5 × daily volume × days in flight ÷ units on the book. Long flights into busy, shallow markets keep little, and short hops into deep, quiet ones keep most. Daily volume is the 30-day average when price history is on, otherwise the last 24 hours. Half of it is assumed to hit the bids.

    2. Freight costs

    Fuel follows the game's mechanics as reverse-engineered by the community (the prun-toolset flight planner). FTL fuel = parsecs flown × reactor power ÷ 333 × reactor usage (7.2 FF per parsec at 100% for a 2,400 reactor), using the game's own jump routes. STL fuel doesn't scale with distance: the approach burns 0.494 × the STL slider × the SF you loaded, and departure burns up to the same. Higher sliders are faster but cost more fuel. Fuel is priced at the departure exchange's real asks. Gateway fees are deducted in the currency they are paid in.

    Choosing the fuel settings

    Faster flights cost more fuel, and slower ones leave the cargo exposed to the market for longer, with more price risk, more of the destination's bids gone, and fewer trips per day. Once you calibrate your ship (see the Ship calibration tab), the tool screens every STL and reactor combination you measured for the best trips, re-solves the most promising ones in full, and tells you which sliders to set.

    3. Choosing the cargo

    Picking what to load is a knapsack problem with three limits at once: cash, weight and volume. It is solved exactly as an integer program (the HiGHS solver, running in your browser), so the answer is often a mix: a dense, valuable good to use the weight limit plus a light, bulky one to fill the space.

    4. Price risk in transit

    A cargo is an unhedged position for the whole flight. Volatility is estimated from 30 days of prices and scaled to the flight time. If the destination price is well above its recent average, part of that premium is assumed to fade before you arrive. Trips are ranked by risk-adjusted profit per hour.

    5. Trading across currencies

    Each faction has its own currency, and the tool assumes you cannot convert between them. A round trip A → B → A is solved as one problem: the return cargo can only be bought with what the first cargo earned at B, profit is measured in your home currency, and cash stranded at B counts as zero.

    Limitations

    • The exchange fee is an estimate. Adjust it to what you see in game. Depth decay assumes trading is spread evenly over the book and ignores new orders placed while you fly.
    • Price moves of different goods are treated as independent, which understates risk if they move together.
    • The order books are a snapshot. The more players arbitrage the same gaps, the smaller they get. That is good for the game's markets.
